# The second version

This document describes the second version of the app: where it is served, what its views show, and where their code lives. The first version is described in [v1.md](v1.md). The two versions share the color scheme setting.

## Addresses

The second version is served under `/v2`. The Historical glottometry view is at `/v2` itself, and the Biological glottometry view at `/v2/biological-glottometry`.

## Historical glottometry

The view draws a glottometric diagram as a living cell. Historical Glottometry, a method of Alexandre François and Siva Kalyan, measures every candidate subgroup of a language family by the innovations its languages share, instead of forcing the family into a tree. A glottometric diagram draws each subgroup as a contour around its languages, nested and overlapping where languages belong to several subgroups.

The view offers a choice of families, each with its citation shown below the scene. It opens on the early Germanic languages:

- six early Germanic languages (Gothic, Old Norse, Old English, Old Frisian, Old Saxon, Old High German), with the 167 shared innovations of Agee's 2018 thesis, read from its Table 1. Languages are labelled by their usual abbreviations in the scene;
- the sample "Demo" family distributed with the Historical Glottometry engine: eighteen fictitious languages, named ⓁA to ⓁR, with 474 shared innovations and the coordinates of each language;
- the six Boni dialects of the Kenyan coast, with the 25 shared innovations of Elias (2019), read from Table 22 of that paper. The paper gives no coordinates.

- **Languages** are bright dots, each labelled at a corner chosen so that labels of nearby languages do not overlap. A family with coordinates is placed by them, with north upward and east to the right, and two arrows at the southwestern corner show those directions, each spanning half a degree. A family without coordinates is placed by likeness, so that languages sharing many innovations lie close together.
- **Subgroups** are translucent lens-shaped membranes enclosing their members. Every set of languages that shares at least one innovation, other than single languages and the whole family, is a candidate subgroup.
- A membrane's **wall is brighter and thicker** the higher the subgroup's subgroupiness.
- Membranes **shade** from one color for the smallest subgroups to another for the largest, and the whole family is one more membrane around everything.
- The whole body **undulates** slowly, so that the diagram reads as a cell with nested organelles rather than a static map.
- **Pointing** at a membrane shows a tooltip naming its subgroup, with its support, cohesiveness and subgroupiness; pointing at a language's dot names the language.

### Scores

For a candidate subgroup, following Kalyan and François (2018):

- **Support** is the number of innovations shared by exactly its members.
- **Cohesiveness** is the share of innovations involving any of its members that do not cut across it. An innovation cuts across a subgroup when it is shared by some but not all of the members together with languages outside it.
- **Subgroupiness** is support multiplied by cohesiveness.

### Controls

- The **Family** menu chooses which family is drawn.
- The **subgroupiness display threshold** slider hides the subgroups whose subgroupiness is below it, as in the online analyzer. It starts at 1.
- The collapsible **Appearance** panel adjusts the shape of the membranes (depth, margins, corner sharpness), their material (body opacity, edge strength and width, and a glassy, satin or matte finish), their colors, and the movement (amplitude, speed, and whether the camera turns on its own). A Reset button restores the defaults.
- The scene can be turned and zoomed with the pointer.
- The **What you are looking at** panel below the scene explains these elements and cites the family's data source and the paper the scores follow.

## Data

Each family's tables live under `src/client/v2/glottometry/` and are parsed in the browser; the view reads nothing from the Worker or the database. `families.ts` registers each family with its name, description and citation.

- `demo/`: the Demo family's two tables, copied verbatim from the engine's sample data (https://marama.huma-num.fr/AF_Glottometry.htm).
- `boni/`: the Boni dialects' innovations, transcribed from the shaded cells of Table 22 in Elias (2019), with each innovation labelled from the paper's own tables. A test checks the transcription against the figures the paper works out for the Bireeri–Safaree pair.
- `germanic/`: the early Germanic innovations, extracted from the checkmarks of Table 1 in Agee (2018) by their positions on the page. A checkmark with an asterisk (a possible parallel development) or a question mark (a complicated classification) is kept as a 1, and the mark is noted in the innovation's label. A test checks the exclusive counts of Anglo-Frisian, Ingvaeonic and Teuto-Saxon against the thesis's Table 3. The thesis's other figures come from case-by-case tallies in its appendices rather than from the binary table, so they differ somewhat from the scores computed here.

The Sogeram study (Daniels, Barth & Barth 2019) prints only a sample of its 196 innovations, so it cannot be added from the paper alone.

## Code

- `src/client/v2/V2App.tsx` lays out the page and routes to the view; `V2Navigation.tsx` lists the tabs.
- `src/client/v2/glottometry/` holds the data and the pure logic: parsing the tables, scoring the subgroups, placing the languages by coordinates or by likeness, and sampling the padded, rounded hull that a membrane is built from. Each file has a unit test beside it.
- `src/client/v2/scene/` holds the view. `SceneView.tsx` holds the threshold, the appearance and the pointed-at subgroup, and lays out the controls, the scene and the key. `CellScene.tsx` is the Three.js canvas, drawn with React Three Fiber, and `CellBody.tsx` places the membranes, nuclei and axes. `undulation.ts` patches the physical material with the shared displacement field and the edge thickening. `appearance.ts` defines the adjustable settings and their defaults, and `controls/` holds the fields the Appearance panel is made of.
- `e2e/versions.spec.ts` checks that the view shows a canvas.

## Biological glottometry

The glottometric diagram read on biological data: the same method as the Historical glottometry view, run on organisms instead of languages. A set of bodies, usually organisms, is drawn as a cell by the same means as a language family, and a chosen relation draws the membranes: where a glottometric membrane is drawn around languages by an innovation they underwent together, here one is drawn around organisms by something they share, which may itself be another organism, a molecule, a word, a story, a place or a taxon. Keeping every relation in one view, over the same sets of bodies, is the point: the same organisms regroup as the relation changes.

The view opens on the plants of Île aux Aigrettes grouped by the visitors they share. Everything the viewer reads is written in the words of the chosen data rather than in general terms: the two menus form the sentence "Show *plants of Île aux Aigrettes, Mauritius* grouped by *the visitors they share*", a caption above the scene says what the dots and the membranes are ("Each dot is a plant. Each membrane wraps the plants that one and the same visitor comes to.") and how far the grouping reaches into the set, and the tooltips and the key are phrased for each grouping in turn. The words for each grouping live in `relations.ts`.

### Dots

The **Show** menu chooses the set of dots, under four headings:

- **From flower surveys**, each giving a set of plants and a set of visitors: Île aux Aigrettes, Mauritius, 1999, and Flores, Azores, 2000 (Olesen, Eskildsen & Venkatasamy 2002; Mangal networks 934 and 935); the oil-collecting bees and Malpighiaceae of Catimbau, Brazil, 2006 (Bezerra, Machado & Mello 2009; Mangal 929); and the spring wildflowers and insects of a Piedmont forest in North Carolina, 1977 to 1979 (Motten 1982; Mangal 945).
- **From food webs**: *Life on a rotting log*, two dozen animals that Savely (1939) found inside dead oak and pine logs in Duke Forest, North Carolina, cut open a year after the trees died and again after three years or more (Mangal networks 1494 to 1497), from springtails and ants that persist through every stage to the bark beetles of fresh pine and the millipedes and snails of softened wood; and *A grassland food web, Jena*, two dozen of the most connected spiders, ground beetles, bugs, rove beetles, woodlice and clovers of the meta-food web that Hines et al. (2019) compiled for the Jena Experiment's grassland plots (Mangal 5013), with the web's fungal node.
- **From other studies**: *Fungi and what they live on*, two dozen fungal genera with named hosts in FungalTraits (Põlme et al. 2020), among them the chains the dataset points at, Tzeanania in Ophiocordyceps in insects, Termitomyces farmed by termites and Chalciporus on Amanita; *Hosts and the fungi on them*, the same table from the hosts' side, from whole kingdoms to single genera; and *Grasses and their borrowed genes*, the thirteen grass genomes in which Hibdige et al. (2021) found genes passed sideways from another lineage of grasses, with six of those lineages and a few relatives the same genes turned up in.
- **From across the database**: *Pines and their company*, the sample's red pine and matsutake with the honey bee, other conifers and the herbs sharing the most compounds with the red pine; *Kitchen plants*, the sixteen crops and herbs with the most compounds listed in Wikidata; and *Fourteen survey sites*, where the bodies are the places of the flower surveys rather than organisms.

Dots are labelled by an abbreviated scientific name, or by a short place name, with the organism's English name beneath in smaller, fainter text where the database has one short enough to fit. They are placed by likeness under the chosen relation, using Jaccard distances so that a dot sharing many things does not stand apart merely by sharing many, and dots the layout puts in the same place are nudged apart. When the grouping changes, the dots glide to their new places over about a second while the membranes fade out and the new ones fade in around them, and the camera moves to keep the whole diagram in view. The scene can be turned by dragging, zoomed by scrolling and moved by dragging with the right button; a **Fit to view** button in the scene's corner brings everything back into view. A dot that shares nothing under the grouping is drawn dim.

Hovering over a membrane raises it, lighter and thicker, mutes the other membranes, and makes its member dots glow larger. Hovering over a dot shows its picture from Wikimedia Commons where the first version's image import found one, with the credit its licence asks for, beneath its English and scientific names.

### Groupings

The **grouped by** menu chooses the relation that draws the membranes. One with nothing recorded among the chosen dots is listed as "none recorded" and cannot be chosen.

- **The visitors they share** and **the flowers they share**: the flower survey read from either side, with each visitor marking the plants it was seen on, or each flower the visitors seen on it. These are weighted by visits counted, so a slider admits only encounters counted at least so many times, and **Replay** starts from the single most frequent encounter and admits rarer ones step by step.
- **The molecules they contain**: Wikidata's "found in taxon" statements. The diagram cannot tell whether two organisms share a molecule by descent, by convergence or by one eating the other.
- **The words people call them by**: a word that occurs in the vernacular names of each organism, from the GBIF backbone and the World Loanword Database.
- **The folktales they appear in**: a motif of Thompson's index that names each organism. A motif told of a whole group reaches every member of the group in the set.
- **The places they were seen**: the flower survey sites at which each organism was observed.
- **Their family tree**: a taxon of the GBIF backbone, from genus to kingdom, containing each organism. This draws only nested membranes, the tree the other relations are read against.
- **The species they share**, for the survey sites: a species observed at several of them.
- **The logs they were found in**, for the rotting log: one kind of log at one stage of decay, so that the four membranes nest and overlap where animals persist as the wood softens. The links are weighted by the years the logs had lain dead, and the weights carry names, so the slider reads "Logs dead up to a year dead" or "three years dead or more". This grouping counts a link when its weight is at most the cutoff rather than at least, so Replay runs forward in time: from the logs a year dead alone to those three years dead or more.
- **The prey they share** and **the predators they share**: the food web read from either side, a prey animal drawn around everything recorded hunting it, or a predator around everything it hunts. For the Jena web the links come only from that web, and for the rotting log only from its four networks, so that GloBI's records of the same species stay out.
- **The food they share** and **the eaters they share**: the same web for what is eaten other than live prey, the fungi, wood, plants, litter and dung recorded as food, drawn around their eaters; or the herbivores, detritivores and omnivores drawn around their foods.
- **The hosts they live on**, for the fungi: the organism FungalTraits names each genus as living on or in, a plant, an insect, another fungus, or a whole kingdom of animals where the source says no more.
- **The fungi that live on them**, for the hosts: a fungal genus drawn around everything it is recorded living on, so the membranes come from the few genera that bridge two hosts.
- **The genes they share by transfer**, for the grasses: one gene passed sideways between grasses, drawn around the grasses it was found in and the lineage it came from, the one grouping that is itself a kind of inheritance cutting across the family tree.

The **scores** are those of Historical Glottometry with a shared thing in the place of an innovation. The key spells out, for each grouping, what a membrane means and how a cross-cutting visitor or molecule differs from a cross-cutting innovation. Hovering over a membrane lists what exactly its members share, as a sentence in the active voice that also says what "exactly these" means ("M. domestica visits these 3 plants and no other plant here"; "The class Magnoliopsida holds these 15 plants and no other plant here"); hovering over a dot names it and counts what it shares ("draws 8 visitors", "contains 23 molecules"). The threshold slider, labelled "Hide membranes weaker than" here, and the Appearance panel are those of the glottometry view, with the threshold starting at 0.

### Data

The sets are copied out of the first version's database by `npm run v2:export-body-sets` (`scripts/exportBodySets.ts`, with the choice of sets in `scripts/bodySetChoices.ts`) into JSON files under `src/client/v2/bodies/sets/`, one per set, with its bodies and, for every relation, the links among them with their weights, quality flags and licences. A relation with nothing recorded is kept with no links, so that the view can say so. The view bundles those files and reads nothing from the Worker. Quality flags are kept in the data but not yet shown.

The food-web, host and gene relations are read by `scripts/bodySetQueries.ts` from the imports described in the README: the rotting-log and Jena sets from `db:import-networks` and `db:import-jena-food-web` (Mangal), the fungi and hosts sets from `db:import-fungal-hosts` (FungalTraits), and the grasses from `db:import-gene-transfers` (Hibdige et al. 2021). Each of these sets names its study, which the key cites under the relations read from it. A sharing whose weights stand for named stages rather than counts, as the rotting log's do, carries a `weightNames` map from each weight to its name ("1" to "a year dead", "3" to "three years dead or more"), which the slider shows in place of the number. A relation's weight words in `relations.ts` say whether a link counts at or above the cutoff, as visits do, or at or below it, as the stages do, and give the sentence the key uses to explain the slider and Replay.

### Code

- `src/client/v2/bodies/` holds the view and its logic. `BodiesView.tsx` holds the chosen set, relation, minimum weight, threshold and appearance, and lays out the sentence of menus, the sliders, the caption, the scene and the key. `relations.ts` gives each relation the phrases every piece of copy is built from, and its explanation. `toSharingTable.ts` reads a set's links under one relation as a glottometric table of shared innovations, so that the glottometry folder's scoring and likeness layout apply unchanged. `spreadApart.ts` keeps dots far enough apart for their labels, `FitCamera.tsx` in the scene folder moves the camera to keep the diagram in view, and `useGlidingPositions.ts` with `glidePositions.ts` moves the dots smoothly between layouts. `BodiesScene.tsx` and `BodiesCell.tsx` draw the cell with the glottometry scene's membranes, nuclei and materials.
- `scoreSubgroups.ts` also lists each subgroup's supporting innovations by label, which the tooltip shows as the things shared by exactly a membrane's members, and `layoutByLikeness.ts` offers Jaccard distances beside the plain count of differing innovations.
